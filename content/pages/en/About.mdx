      Taller Entre Aguas (TEA) is Black Puerto Rican microlab in the Community Knowledge Lab, an archipelago of the Diaspora Solidarities Lab (dslprojects.org). TEA is comprised of three main projects: The Registro, the Criadas Project, and the Libertos Project. We are interested in expanding the ways Black Puerto Ricans gather and think together towards liberation, progress and action. These forms may include methodologies, curriculum, and other documentations for public use that offer best practices for decolonial work. We also aim to engage digital humanities through a diasporic, Black and ethnic studies approach.

      * We fully support the Black feminist principles of the Diaspora Solidarities Lab (DSL).
      * Our projects and research will be made accessible in Puerto Rican Spanish and English translation, not because veneration of Spanish imperial oppressors, but because our targeted community speaks these languages due to the compounded pressure of Indigenous genocide, African slavery, colonialism, U.S. imperialism, and First World globalization.
      * We are interested in expanding the ways Black Puerto Ricans gather and think together towards liberation, progress and action. These forms may include methodologies, curriculum, and other documentations for public use and that offer best practices for doing decolonial and diasporic Black and ethnic studies digital humanities work.
      * We will include the formation of knowledge communities and make known the protagonist role of Black Puerto Rican people and explore the important elements of the knowledge of our ancestors' culture, which should be incorporated into the current teaching of different academic fields with the purpose of valuing every single life.

      Despite being a group that has been invisibilized by historiography in many ways, Black Puerto Ricans have managed to introduce elements of their culture in every country they were put in. Centuries of ecological, political, economic, and social crisis stems from the conditions of colonial capitalism position Puerto Rican’s present and future. Whether in the island or diaspora, many Puerto Ricans face themselves for generations negotiating structures of colonial violence and targeted organized abandonment that seek to unmake bodies and relationships. Under these conditions, Puerto Ricans are coerced into either embracing a resilience narrative promoted by a neoliberal state or succumbing and conforming to the “no future” premised by the state’s neglect. We are interested in amplifying Black Puerto Ricans’ future. Charting new methods in archival research, theory, and community practice, we join to address how the humanities can deepen its commitment to community.
